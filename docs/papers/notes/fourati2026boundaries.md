## Summary

A conceptual paper that attacks the assumption underneath most
autonomy research: that humans remain in the loop only because AI is
not yet capable enough. The authors ask instead where automation's
*conceptual* limits lie, and argue human participation can persist
even with arbitrarily capable systems, for three distinct reasons.
**Technical or complementarity** grounds: humans supply capabilities
or perspectives the system lacks. **Normative or developmental**
grounds: participating is itself valuable for human agency or
learning, independent of output quality. **Emergence** grounds, which
they treat as the most important: in some activities the target is not
specified in advance but emerges through the interaction, so
participation is constitutive of what is being produced rather than a
means of producing it. Human–AI co-construction is therefore a
permanent feature of such activities, not a transitional state.

## Contribution

A taxonomy of reasons for persistent human involvement that separates
capability arguments from constitutive ones. The third category is the
novel move: it makes "keep a human in the loop" a claim about the
nature of the task rather than about the current state of the
technology. Being a position paper, it offers argument rather than
evidence.

## Method

Conceptual/theoretical. No experiments, no data. Strength rests on
whether the three grounds are genuinely distinct and whether target
emergence is real in the activities the reader cares about.

## Relevance to RISE

This is the theoretical counterweight to the `autonomy_level`
dimension of the evaluation rubric. The catalog scores systems on how
far they operate without human approval, with higher autonomy
implicitly the direction of travel; this paper argues that for some
research activities the ceiling is not a capability frontier at all.
That matters for reading entries such as `pai-econ-claude` (gated
human checkpoints by design) and for the curator's own
human-approval-by-default stance — it supplies the argument for why
that is a design position rather than a temporary safeguard. Research
itself is a strong candidate for target emergence: the research
question is frequently reformulated through the work, which is exactly
the case the authors describe.

## Critique / open questions

No empirical content, so the claim that target emergence actually
characterises research (as opposed to being merely plausible) is not
demonstrated. The three grounds may be less separable in practice than
in the taxonomy — a complementarity argument can usually be
reformulated as a capability gap that closes later, which is precisely
the move the paper wants to block. Whether any of this survives
contact with systems that reformulate their own objectives is open.
